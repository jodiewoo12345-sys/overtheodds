Our rules

Everything the board does, written down. No secret sauce, because the rules aren't the clever bit — doing them the same way every single day is.

If you want the idea behind it rather than the settings, start here instead.

What gets on the board

Twice a day we collect prices from every UK bookmaker we can reach, work out what the market as a whole thinks each outcome is worth, and list anything priced generously against that. A bet only appears if it clears all of these:

RuleWhat it means
At least 5 bookmakers If fewer than five firms have priced a market, we ignore it. Two or three bookmakers aren't a market, they're a rumour.
At least 2% better The price has to beat what the market says by this much. Anything smaller is within the noise.
Nothing above 6.00 Long shots carry enormous margin and the market agrees with itself least on them, so the maths is least trustworthy exactly where it looks most exciting.
Nothing above 12% In a market with a dozen firms, a genuine 15% edge doesn't exist. If our sums produce one, our sums are wrong, so we bin it.
Starting in over 2 hours Prices move fastest near the off. If we can't give you a fair chance of still getting it, we don't list it.
Same line only Over 2.5 goals at one firm and over 3.5 at another are different bets. We only compare firms offering the same thing.
One row per bet Where several firms offer the same selection, we show the best price and list the others underneath, rather than filling the page with the same bet.

Where the "market price" comes from

Every bookmaker's prices add up to more than certainty, and the extra is their cut. We strip that out of each firm's prices separately, which leaves their honest view of the chances. Then we take the middle of all those views.

Two details that matter. We leave a firm out of the calculation when we're judging that same firm, or a generous price would drag the average towards itself and hide the thing we're looking for. And we use the betting exchanges in the calculation, because their prices are sharp, but we never list an exchange as a bet — they charge commission and plenty of people don't hold an account.

How we check whether any of it works

Between about 45 and 3 minutes before each event starts, we go back and look at what the whole market has settled on. That's the fairest test available: did the price we published beat the price the market ended up at?

We take the closest check we manage. If the market had already closed, or too few firms were left pricing it, we record that the check failed and leave the bet out of the figures entirely. We never estimate a price we didn't see.

This is a better early test than profit. Profit over fifty bets is luck. Beating the market's final price, consistently, is the thing that eventually turns into profit.

How results are settled

OutcomeHow we count it
WonProfit at the price we published, on a notional £10.
LostMinus the £10.
PushStake back. It counts as neither a winner nor a loser, exactly as a bookmaker would treat it. Lines like over 2.0 goals finishing 1-1 do this.
Anything elseLeft out of every figure and flagged on the record page as unrecognised, rather than quietly counted as a loss.

Results come from the same data provider as the prices, and nothing is settled by hand.

What we never do

We don't remove a bet from the record because it lost. We don't change the price we published after the event. We don't drop a bad month, a bad sport or a bad week. Every selection that goes on the board ends up in the record, win or lose.

If we change how any of this works — a different threshold, a new sport, a change to the sums — the date and the settings are stored against every bet, so the before and after can be compared rather than blended together.

What this is not. These aren't tips. We have no idea who wins. We only know which prices look generous against the rest of the market, and we might be wrong about that. There are losing weeks and losing months in any approach like this, and no amount of good process changes it.

Bookmakers also restrict or close accounts, usually the ones that cost them money. Holding accounts with several firms softens that, but it happens to most people eventually.
